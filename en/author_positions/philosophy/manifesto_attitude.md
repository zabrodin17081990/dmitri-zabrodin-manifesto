# The Author's Attitude Toward the Manifesto

---

*(stable)*

## Thesis
The author does not seek mass or rapid dissemination of the manifesto's ideas and approaches them with extreme caution.

## The danger of breaking the system
Human society is a complex, dynamically balanced system. Religion, anthropocentrism, traditional morals and ethics — despite their illusion and relativity — act as "crutches" or load-bearing structures that maintain this system's stability over millennia.

Rapid and widespread adoption of the manifesto's ideas could lead to catastrophic consequences:
1.  **Crisis of meaning:** Realizing oneself as a deterministic algorithm in a finite world may produce mass depression, apathy, or a choice of "non-existence" by some people.
2.  **Moral crisis:** Understanding the relativity of ethical norms can be misinterpreted as their abolition. That may trigger waves of crime, looting, and social chaos.
3.  **Civil conflict:** As a result, society may split between those who attempt to preserve order and those who use the new understanding as an excuse for destructive interaction.

Personal comfort for the author, as for most people, would inevitably collapse in such a scenario. Posthumous recognition in a ruined world has no value.

## Analogy with the "sparrow campaign"
Attempting to rapidly change a complex system without understanding its interdependencies (like the campaign to exterminate sparrows in China in the 1950s–60s) leads to unpredictable and often opposite outcomes.

## Conclusion
The manifesto is not a call for revolution but an invitation to slow, careful, individual reflection. These ideas should spread evolutionarily, gradually integrating into cultural code rather than detonating it from within. The existing ecosystem, morals, and culture of Humanity and other forms of Consciousness are balanced for a reason — they are the result of millennia of natural selection that produced a relatively stable state.
