# Language Issues in Defining Consciousness

---
*(stub)*

This document captures reflections on the terminological confusion arising from discussions of consciousness and the conventions adopted within this manifesto.

## The Problem of Double Meaning

In both Russian and English, the key term "Consciousness" carries a dual semantic load, which is the source of most debates and misunderstandings.

1.  **Consciousness as Process:** Denotes the mechanism, the "hardware". This is synonymous with wakefulness, attention, the mental space where information is processed.
    *   *Examples:* "come to consciousness", "a thought flickered in my consciousness", "the contents of consciousness".

2.  **Consciousness as Product (Entity):** Denotes the phenomenon itself, the emergent property of "being", subjective experience. This is what the manifesto defines as "Living in the ontological sense".
    *   *Examples:* "the mystery of human consciousness", "the hard problem of consciousness".

## Confusion in the English Language

In English, the problem is compounded by the existence of several overlapping terms:

*   **Consciousness:** Used in both senses (process and product).
*   **Awareness:** Closer to the process, to the act of directing attention.
*   **Mind:** A broader concept, including both conscious and unconscious processes.
*   **Self-awareness vs. Self-consciousness:** The first more often means neutral self-recognition as an object. The second often carries a negative connotation of shyness, excessive self-reflection.

## Adopted Convention

For clarity and rigor, the following agreements are adopted within this manifesto:

1.  **Attention (Consciousness as process):** Used to denote the mechanism, the "active attention zone", the information processing process.
2.  **Self-consciousness:** Used to denote the emergent property, the product of the mechanism's work. Denotes the state of "Living in the ontological sense" — a system possessing a "self" model and operating with it.
3.  **Consciousness (flexible term):** The use of this general term is permitted, but with mandatory clarification in parentheses to resolve ambiguity. For example: `Consciousness (as an active attention zone)` or `Consciousness (in the ontological sense)`.

This convention allows us to preserve the naturalness of language while providing the precision needed for analysis.
